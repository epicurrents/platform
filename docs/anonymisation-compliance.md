# Anonymisation compliance

Whether anything this platform produces is anonymous data, assessed against the European Data Protection Board's Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation, and what follows for how the platform's de-identification must be described, granted and documented. Audience: the deployment operator (the controller), the person writing the deployment's privacy notice or data-sharing agreements, and developers touching any surface that serves recording data. It is the anonymisation lens of [gdpr-compliance.md](gdpr-compliance.md), which remains the inventory of personal data, retention and erasure; this document does not repeat that inventory.

The short answer, stated first because every other section depends on it: **the platform pseudonymises recordings; it does not anonymise them.** What a grantee, a share-token holder or a federated peer receives under a de-identifying grant is personal data in the platform's hands and, absent a documented per-recipient assessment, must be treated as personal data in the recipient's hands too. Nothing in the software, its API responses, its user interface or its documentation may describe that output as anonymous. The reasons are in [the three criteria applied](#the-three-criteria-applied); the consequences for wording, granting and documentation follow it; the design gaps the assessment found are in [design gaps](#design-gaps).

## Sources

**Primary.** EDPB, *Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation*, version 1.0, adopted for public consultation on 7 July 2026 (published on edpb.europa.eu as `edpb_guidelines_202602_anonymisation_v1_en_0.pdf`). Paragraph references in this document (¶ *n*) are to that version. It replaces the Article 29 Working Party's Opinion 05/2014 on anonymisation techniques and restates the three criteria of that opinion as *No Record Isolation*, *No Linkage* and *No Inference* (¶ 2–3, 52). Two things about its status matter here. It is a consultation draft, so paragraph numbers and some wording may move before final adoption; re-check the references when the final version appears. And its footnote 3 says an assessment done under Opinion 05/2014 need not be redone, which does not help this platform: no anonymisation assessment had been done under either instrument before this document.

**Secondary**, in the order this document relies on them:

- Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR). Recital 26 and Art. 4(1) define personal and anonymous data; Art. 4(5) defines pseudonymisation; Art. 9 makes the signal data special-category health data; Art. 25 and Art. 32(1)(a) name pseudonymisation as a design and security measure rather than an exemption; Art. 89(1) makes pseudonymisation the expected safeguard for research processing where the purpose can still be met; Art. 5(1)(c) and (e) bound what may be kept.
- The CJEU case law the guidelines rest on: C-413/23 P *EDPS v SRB* (anonymity is assessed from each relevant entity's perspective, and data the transferring entity can identify is personal for the transfer, ¶ 6–7, 21), C-582/14 *Breyer* (means reasonably likely to be used, including through third parties), C-319/22 *Scania* (identification across a chain of entities), C-479/22 P *OC v Commission* (the recipient population's skills set the perspective, ¶ 12), C-604/22 *IAB Europe* (additional information).
- EDPB, *Guidelines 01/2025 on Pseudonymisation* (consultation version, January 2025), for the boundary the anonymisation guidelines draw at ¶ 91: a reversible transformation is a safeguard, not anonymisation, and the pseudonymising controller stays a controller of personal data.
- Regulation (EU) 2025/327 on the European Health Data Space. Its secondary-use chapter is not yet applicable and a single-institution deployment is unlikely to be a health data holder under it, but it fixes the vocabulary a health-data platform will be measured against: access is given in anonymised form where the purpose allows and pseudonymised form otherwise, re-identification by the data user is prohibited, and the assessment of which form applies is the access body's, not the requester's.
- Regulation (EU) 2022/868 (Data Governance Act), Art. 5, for the same anonymise-or-pseudonymise-and-isolate pattern applied to protected public-sector data, which is where a hospital-held recording archive would sit.

Neither of the two Regulations changes the analysis below; they are cited because a deployment that describes its output as anonymous to satisfy one of them inherits exactly the burden of proof the guidelines describe.

## The legal test, mapped onto the platform

The guidelines ask two questions of any given data (¶ 6): does it *relate* to a natural person, and is that person *identified or identifiable* using means reasonably likely to be used? Only a "no" to either makes the data anonymous, and both answers may differ from one entity to another (¶ 7). A recording relates to its subject by content in the most direct sense the guidelines describe: it is "a patient's medical record" (¶ 16(a)). The first question is therefore never in doubt, and the whole assessment turns on the second, which the guidelines treat as one of likelihood rather than possibility (¶ 8, 22): the likelihood of identification must be *insignificant in reality*, from the perspective of every relevant entity, taking into account means available through third parties (¶ 20, 28).

### Relevant entities

Anonymity is assessed per entity, from the perspective of those for whom the data is intended to be anonymous (¶ 11–12), including unauthorised and malicious ones where they could plausibly reach the data (¶ 30). For this platform the entities are:

| Entity | How it reaches the data | What it holds besides |
|---|---|---|
| The deployment operator and its staff, including whoever has database or host access | Everything: the stored file, the database, the backups, the write-only originals volume where `RECORDINGS_PRESERVE_MODE` is on | Whatever the `source_*` columns, the "Original annotations" rows and `original_name` carry; on a clinical deployment, the institution's own records of who was recorded when |
| The recording's author | Raw bytes on every serving path (the `apply_middleware` flag is ignored for the author), `original_name`, `processing_error`, the `source_*` channel fields | Typically the identity of the subject, since they exported the file from the acquisition system |
| A local grantee reading under a de-identifying grant, directly or through a dataset | The served bytes after the pipeline, the full metadata response, annotation rows with text withheld | Whatever their role gives them: a colleague at the same institution usually holds the means to link |
| A share-token holder | The same as a de-identifying grantee, without authenticating, for as long as the token lives, unless the token was minted raw | Unknown by construction; the link can be forwarded |
| A federated peer instance and its users | The same as a de-identifying grantee plus `download_size`; raw only under an explicit `--no-apply-middleware` grant | A separate controller with its own population, including, in the common case of two hospitals sharing, clinicians who may have treated the subject |
| The acquiring institution, whether or not it is the operator | Usually not the platform's copy at all | The original file, its acquisition log, and the subject's identity: the entity every linkage in this document ultimately runs to |
| Whoever obtains the data by breach: a stolen backup, a compromised peer, an intercepted share link | Whatever the breached store holds | Unbounded; ¶ 30 lists cybercriminals and foreign intelligence explicitly and ¶ 33 says a legal prohibition is no barrier for entities it does not bind |

Two rules from the guidelines govern how these combine. **The chain of means** (¶ 20, 28, footnote 21): if a recipient could reasonably obtain additional information from, or hand the data to, another entity that can identify the subject, that route counts as the recipient's own means. A peer institution that can ask the acquiring institution "is this yours" has the means. **Onward transfer** (¶ 21): if it is reasonably likely that an entity can pass the data to a third party who could identify the subject, the data is personal for that transfer and, indirectly, for the transferring entity. A share token whose link can be forwarded, and a peer whose users can download, both satisfy that condition.

"Lack of motivation" is not a factor the guidelines allow (¶ 31), and a contractual prohibition is not a prohibition by law (¶ 34): a federation trust relationship, a data-sharing agreement or the terms shown to a share-token holder can complement technical measures but cannot on their own move the answer. The one legal prohibition that does count, ¶ 32, is one that binds the entities actually involved; a data-user re-identification ban of the EHDS kind would count for institutions under EU jurisdiction, and for no one else.

### Where the platform stands before the criteria are applied

The guidelines recommend mapping the given data and any additional information before applying the criteria, because some cases are settled at that step (¶ 50–51). One such case applies here. **From the operator's own perspective the data is personal data by simple inspection**: the operator holds the author's raw copy, the `source_*` originals, the "Original annotations" bundle written from the file's own annotation channel, `original_name` where `RECORDINGS_DISCARD_ORIGINAL_NAME` is off, and the preserved original where preservation is on. The de-identification pass is a transformation applied to a copy while the platform keeps the means to reverse it, which is the definition of pseudonymisation in Art. 4(5) GDPR and the case ¶ 91 distinguishes from anonymisation. This is also what ¶ 37 warns about: data made anonymous for a recipient but not for the anonymising controller leaves that controller with every GDPR obligation intact, including the ones in [gdpr-compliance.md](gdpr-compliance.md).

The question the rest of this document answers is therefore the narrower one: is the served output anonymous *for a recipient*? The guidelines offer two ways to answer it (¶ 45–49). The **simplified approach** disregards differences between entities and asks whether re-identification is possible in theory; passing it is sufficient for anonymity, failing it is not conclusive. The **contextual approach** assesses each relevant entity's means. This document applies the simplified approach, because the platform is software and cannot know its deployments' recipients; a deployment that wants to claim anonymity for a specific recipient takes the contextual route described in [what a contextual assessment would need](#what-a-contextual-assessment-would-need).

## The three criteria applied

Under the simplified approach, against the given data above. The framing the guidelines give for what makes a technique effective applies with full force to this data: re-identification "is more likely to be successful against record-level data with high dimensionality and high resolution" (¶ 86), and an EEG recording is record-level data of the highest dimensionality and resolution the platform could hold, one record per subject, tens of channels, hundreds of samples per second, for minutes to days.

### No Record Isolation

The criterion is met if the data contains no unique combination of attribute values relating to a single individual (¶ 55). **It fails.** Every recording is a record in the guidelines' sense, all values known to relate to one individual (¶ 5, glossary), and every recording is unique: no two signal files share sample values, and even the header numerics alone (exact duration to the record, channel set, calibration ranges, filter values) are unlikely to collide across a deployment. The guidelines note that a larger record with more attributes is more likely to be unique (¶ 56); this is the limiting case.

Failing this criterion is not conclusive on its own (¶ 52, 99). The follow-up question at ¶ 98 is whether the isolated record allows *singling out*: matching its attributes against attributes of the individual available to the relevant entity. Example 24 (¶ 99) describes the case where uniqueness does not lead to identification: a secret-ballot survey whose answers cannot be matched to anything about the respondent. A recording is not that case. Its attributes match, exactly, one thing: the original file held by the acquiring institution and by the author, which is where the next criterion goes.

### No Linkage

The criterion is met if no record could be linked, with certainty or high likelihood, to a record about the same individual in a different dataset (¶ 60). **It fails, on several independent routes**, in decreasing order of directness:

1. **`file_hash` is a bit-exact link to the original.** The metadata response serves the SHA-256 of the uploaded bytes before any de-identification. Any entity holding a candidate original, which is the acquiring institution, the author, and anyone the export passed through, can confirm with certainty that a served recording is that subject's by hashing their copy. This is a common identifier in the sense of ¶ 62 shared between the platform's copy and the original, and it also answers the membership-inference question of ¶ 79 with no analysis at all. It is the one route the platform created rather than inherited, and it is closable; see [design gaps](#design-gaps).
2. **The signal is its own identifier.** Absent the hash, the samples still match the original bit-for-bit, and match it by correlation after any resampling or filtering. Linking a served file to the source archive needs no more than the source archive, and `stored_hash` makes that join cheaper for a holder of the original who can reproduce the platform's pass bit-exactly: one digest comparison in place of a correlation. For everyone else it links to nothing outside the platform, which is the route `file_hash` opened and this digest does not. The guidelines' example of a linkage-resistant dataset is physiological states "collected for many patients" that "vary over time" (¶ 65); a specific recording is the opposite, a fixed trace of one session.
3. **Timing and geometry.** `created_at` is the upload timestamp, which on a clinical deployment falls within hours of acquisition and so reinstates, at coarser resolution, the start date the header pass deletes. The exact duration and record count, the event timestamps that survive text withholding (since 2026-09-24 including one per event the file arrived with, translated or not), and the interruption geometry match the acquisition log and the source annotation file. Together they are a robust record-linkage key of the kind ¶ 64 describes as surviving even noisy data.
4. **Site and device fingerprint.** The residual header numerics, channel set and mains notch narrow the acquiring site and hardware, as [channel-deidentification-plan.md](engineering-notes/channel-deidentification-plan.md) records under residual risk. This is a pool-shrinker rather than a link, but ¶ 24 and Example 5 are about exactly that: attributes that are not unique alone become unique in combination.
5. **Free text the platform warns about but does not decide on.** `display_name` is set by the uploader, or by anyone with write access through the PATCH and collection bulk-rename endpoints; dataset names and descriptions, folder names and tag names are typed the same way, and all are shown to recipients as typed. Since 2026-09-23 the write endpoints return a warning when the text reads as a person's name, a run of digits, a date or one of the deployment's own patterns, push bodies name a recording by its handle only, and tags are listed by reach rather than to everyone; the text itself is still stored and shown as typed, so the notice says these fields are the uploader's responsibility.

The consequence under ¶ 101–103 is that the linked information, the platform's copy joined to the acquiring institution's original, directly identifies the subject. The linkage does not need the platform's help, and the guidelines are explicit that the availability of the linking dataset to a *third* party in the chain is enough (¶ 20, 28).

### No Inference

The criterion is met if no specific and meaningful inference can be drawn from the data (¶ 67): an inference about one identifiable individual that affects their rights and interests, relies on the data, and could not be drawn from general knowledge. **It fails.** The clinical content of a recording is what it is for. A reader can infer epileptiform activity, sleep architecture, encephalopathy, medication effects and age band from the trace; those inferences are specific (they concern the one person recorded), meaningful (they are health data and are drawn from this record, not from the population, which is the test at ¶ 70–71), and they attach to the record in the sense of ¶ 74. Where the No Linkage failure identifies the subject, the inference is about a named person; where it does not, it is about a record that the previous criterion shows can be singled out.

The criterion also fails in the narrower, technical way ¶ 79 describes: `file_hash` allows membership inference, confirming that a particular original was uploaded, for anyone holding the original.

### Result

Under the simplified approach all three criteria fail, and the follow-up analysis at ¶ 98–103 does not rescue any of them. The table at ¶ 97 gives the outcome: the data "should not be considered anonymous" under the simplified approach, and a deployment that wants a different answer for a specific recipient must continue to the contextual approach. Until it does, and documents the result, the served output is **pseudonymised personal data** for every recipient, and special-category personal data at that.

This matches the two places in the repository that had already drawn the line correctly: the channel de-identification plan ("pool-shrinking mitigation, not anonymisation... no user-facing text should claim otherwise") and the BIDS export design ("Pseudonymised, not anonymised... an Art. 32 safeguard, not an exemption"). It contradicts the vocabulary used almost everywhere else, which [vocabulary](#vocabulary) addresses.

## What a contextual assessment would need

A deployment may legitimately conclude that a particular recording, or a particular grant, is anonymous *for a particular recipient* (¶ 96), and a research collaboration is the case where that conclusion is most plausible: Example 4 (¶ 15) and Example 22 (¶ 94) are both research institutes receiving clinical data they cannot link. The guidelines' flowchart in Annex 1 is the procedure. What it would require of this platform, in order:

1. **Fix the platform-created linkage first.** No contextual argument survives `file_hash` in the response, because the argument would have to establish that the recipient can never obtain the original, and a hash lets them test that proposition against any original that reaches them later. The [design gaps](#design-gaps) entries for `file_hash`, `created_at` and free-text hygiene are prerequisites, not refinements.
2. **Name the recipient and everyone behind them** (¶ 12–13, 30): the peer institution's users, whoever they can forward to, whoever can compel them. A peer that is also a treating institution for the subject population is disqualified at this step, since its clinicians hold the linking information; so is any recipient population that overlaps the acquiring institution's.
3. **Establish that the recipient cannot reach the original by means reasonably likely to be used** (¶ 26, 29): no shared archive, no legal right of access to the source records, no plausible collusion with the source institution, and, per ¶ 33, no evidence that whatever prohibition applies is routinely disregarded. A written agreement helps only as a complement (¶ 34).
4. **Assess the signal itself as an identifier for that recipient**, which is the step no header rewrite touches. The relevant means are a reference recording of the same subject and the biometric-identification techniques of the current state of the art, which ¶ 84 and ¶ 92 require to be considered and which ¶ 35 requires to be re-checked as they improve. For a recipient with no reference recordings the likelihood may be insignificant; for a treating institution it is not.
5. **Decide the inference question for that recipient** (¶ 87): the clinical inferences are drawn either way, and the question is whether they attach to an identifiable person for this recipient. If steps 3 and 4 hold, they do not.
6. **Apply the onward-transfer rule** (¶ 21): the conclusion is void if the recipient is reasonably likely to pass the data on to someone for whom steps 3 and 4 do not hold. A share token fails this step by construction, since its link is transferable and its holder is unknown; a federated grant to a named institution can pass it.
7. **Write it down and date it** (¶ 41), keep it with the grant, and reassess on the triggers in [reassessment](#reassessment). The grant has a place for it: `AccessRight.assessment_reference` and `assessment_date`, settable on every grant surface and shown to the sharer, the object's author and superusers only, so the grantee never learns whether a finding about them exists. The document may be a third party's: for recordings taken from a published dataset, the publisher's anonymity statement is the natural one, since a recipient who can fetch the same data from the publisher gains no means from the platform's copy (¶ 26), and the sharer adopts that conclusion as their own finding, re-checked like any other. Since 2026-09-24 the recording itself says where it came from: `Recording.public_source` holds the DOI or URL, `deidentification_report` repeats it per recording and `grant_assessments` counts, per grant, how many of the covered recordings carry one, so a sharer can see at once whether the publisher's statement covers everything a grant serves. The platform checks the locator's shape and nothing else; the assertion is the author's. ¶ 88 asks for safety margins because capabilities change; a conclusion with no margin is a conclusion that expires unnoticed, which is what `grant_assessments` is for.

A deployment that completes these steps for a recipient has anonymous data for that recipient, and personal data for itself (¶ 37). It has not changed what the platform is, and the platform's own descriptions do not change with it: the software still ships pseudonymised output, and the anonymity is a property of that deployment's finding, not of the code.

## Consequences for the deployment

These follow from ¶ 37–42 and from the result above, and they are the operator's obligations rather than the software's.

**The de-identification pass is itself processing of special-category data** (¶ 38). Ingest reads the identifying header, the annotation channel and the signal in order to rewrite them; that processing needs an Art. 6 basis and an Art. 9(2) condition, presumed to be the same as those of the upload it belongs to when the purpose is the same. A deployment whose position is that "no patient personal data reaches the platform" is asserting that the Art. 9 processing happened before upload, at the client, and that the four ingest privacy overrides in [recordings/README.md](../recordings/README.md#ingest-privacy-overrides) merely enforce that. That position is only as good as the client-side step it relies on and the overrides being on; with the defaults, the platform retains the uploaded filename, the embedded annotation text and the source channel metadata, and the position is false for every recording that arrived un-anonymised.

**Transparency wording** (¶ 40). Controllers "should not use descriptions like 'anonymous', 'de-identified' or 'de-personalised' if individuals are actually still identifiable." The privacy notice must describe sharing as pseudonymised, name what is removed and what is not, and say that the recipient may not attempt re-identification. [privacy-notice-template.md](privacy-notice-template.md) has been brought into line with this document; the operator's copy in `local/` needs the same change if it predates it.

**Contractual terms with recipients** are complements, not controls (¶ 34, 90). A federation peer agreement and any research collaboration agreement should carry a re-identification prohibition and a no-onward-transfer clause, and the operator should treat them as reducing likelihood at step 3 of a contextual assessment, never as establishing anonymity on their own.

**Documentation of the process** (¶ 41). The controller must be able to show what was done to each recording and that it was effective. The platform stamps each recording with the version of the de-identification pass that wrote it and whether its annotation text was kept, seals those with the ingest privacy overrides in force into the tamper-evident audit row of the READY transition, and audits every serving decision with its `apply_middleware` value; `deidentification_report` presents the record per recording and flags recordings written by an older pass than the running code. Recordings processed before 2026-09-23 carry version `0` and no sealed record, so for them this document and the deployment's `.env` at the time of ingest remain the record, and the operator should keep dated copies of both.

**Retention of originals** (¶ 10, 39). The guidelines are neutral on whether the original is deleted, but deletion is what lets anonymisation count towards storage limitation and weigh in a legitimate-interest balance. The write-only originals volume, the `source_*` columns and the "Original annotations" rows are each a decision to keep the reversal key. A deployment that keeps none of them still holds the author's raw copy, so the decision is about how many keys exist, not whether one does.

## Reassessment

The guidelines require reassessment when capabilities change (¶ 35, 88) and after a security incident that touched information the assessment relied on (¶ 42). For this platform the triggers are:

- **Any change to the [given data](#the-given-data) tables**: a field added to `RecordingOut`, `RecordingMetaOut` or `SignalInfoOut`, a new byte-serving or export endpoint, a new share surface, a change to what the redaction helper withholds. The `phi-exposure` review agent fires on these; its finding should include whether this document's tables still hold.
- **Any security incident** involving the originals volume, a backup, the database, a peer's credentials or a share token in circulation. The incident may hand an entity the linking information a contextual assessment assumed it lacked, which is the case ¶ 42 describes, and it may also engage Art. 33 and 34 notification. The operator runbook's [after-an-incident step](operator-runbook.md#after-a-security-incident) lists every assessed grant with `grant_assessments` for the sharers to re-run.
- **A recording re-written by the de-identification pass** after the assessment of a grant covering it, directly or as a dataset member: the assessment looked at different output than the recipient now receives. `grant_assessments` reports such grants as `reprocessed`, from the date of the sealed record in the READY audit row, and `dataset_anonymity_report` flags such a member at the release it belongs to.
- **The six-monthly GDPR sweep** in [gdpr-compliance.md](gdpr-compliance.md#keeping-this-document-current) includes this lens, and for a release-gated dataset runs `dataset_access_report` for the window and `dataset_anonymity_report` for every release: re-read the criteria against the current serving surfaces, re-check the state of the art for EEG subject identification, and confirm any contextual assessments a deployment has on file still hold. `grant_assessments --due` lists the ones older than half a year or overtaken by a re-processing.
- **A change on the anonymisation path** that is not a given-data change: the release gate, the submission gate, the dataset reports, the assessment record, the published-source field, the de-identification record or the text-hygiene heuristic. The `anonymisation` review agent fails such a diff unless this document's log carries a row for it; the row may say the classification is unchanged, and usually does.
- **The review date in the [currency](#currency) block passing.** The `anonymisation` agent fails every commit from that day until the block is moved, `manage.py check` warns on every deploy, and `grant_assessments` says so at the head of the sweep. The pressure is deliberate: a document that is only re-read when someone remembers to is not one a controller can point to.
- **Final adoption of the guidelines**, to update the paragraph references and absorb any change to the criteria. The [currency](#currency) block's version moves first, which fails the agent's pin and the contract test until the references are re-read.

## Vocabulary

Because of ¶ 40, the platform's own language is a compliance surface. The rule for everything user-facing, operator-facing and in documentation:

- **De-identified** and **pseudonymised** describe what the pipeline does. "De-identified" names the mechanism (identifiers removed); "pseudonymised" names the legal status (Art. 4(5), reversible by the controller). Either is accurate; "pseudonymised" is the one a privacy notice or a data-sharing agreement should use because it carries the correct legal consequence.
- **Anonymised** and **anonymous** describe a finding a deployment has documented for a recipient under the [contextual approach](#what-a-contextual-assessment-would-need). The software never has that finding and never uses the words for its own output.
- **Header identification fields blanked** is the accurate description of what `_build_clean_header` does to the header; "anonymised header" overstates it, since the header still carries the geometry that links.

Since 2026-09-24 the `anonymisation` review agent applies the rule to every diff: a new occurrence of the words describing the platform's output in a user-facing string, an API message, a notice or a document is a finding, with the exemptions (the guidelines themselves, a deployment's finding, the identifiers that name the anonymity report, and the unauthenticated-caller sense of "anonymous") listed in [.review/exemptions/anonymisation.md](../.review/exemptions/anonymisation.md). The rule was applied across the repository on 2026-09-22: code, tests, UI strings and documentation say de-identified or pseudonymised, `AnonymizeEDFHeader` became `DeidentifyEDFHeader` with the old name kept as an import alias for project pipelines, and the published data-protection page was edited in the docs submodule for its next release. The remaining occurrences of the word are this document, the EDPB references, and the places that say what the platform does *not* do. The execution plan for the other gaps is [engineering-notes/anonymisation-compliance-plan.md](engineering-notes/anonymisation-compliance-plan.md).

## Design gaps

What the assessment found, in order of consequence. Each is a ROADMAP entry under the `Privacy` prefix; this table is the assessment's view of them, the ROADMAP is where their status lives, and [engineering-notes/anonymisation-compliance-plan.md](engineering-notes/anonymisation-compliance-plan.md) is the phased plan for closing them.

| Gap | Why it matters under the guidelines | What closes it |
|---|---|---|
| **Closed 2026-09-22.** `file_hash` (SHA-256 of the pre-de-identification upload) was served to every grantee, share-token holder and federated peer in `RecordingOut` and the slice response, and shown in the frontend details panel | A bit-exact linkage and membership-inference key to the original (¶ 62, 79). It is the only linkage route the platform created rather than inherited, and it defeats any contextual assessment in advance, since the recipient can test any original they later obtain against it | Remove `file_hash` from every response, the author's included; nobody needs it on the wire, and any endpoint that answers whether a hash exists is a membership oracle whoever asks. If a public fingerprint is needed for de-duplication or cache keys, expose a digest of the *stored* bytes instead, which is a hash of already-pseudonymised content and links to nothing outside the platform for anyone who does not hold the original (a holder who can re-run the pass bit-exactly still has a join, cheaper than correlating samples, which no digest of the stored bytes can close). The duplicate-detection ROADMAP entry can keep using the original-bytes hash internally. Done as written: no response carries `file_hash`, the author's included; `Recording.stored_hash` is the served digest; `backfill_stored_hash` stamps older recordings; the key set per reader is pinned by a load-bearing contract test |
| **Closed 2026-09-22.** `created_at` (upload time) was served exact to every reader | On a clinical deployment it is the acquisition date at hours' resolution, reinstating what the header pass removed (¶ 63, Example 12: generalised attributes still link). The file-timestamp normalisation at ingest shows the designers knew this and closed the filesystem side only | Withhold or coarsen it for non-author readers (a month is enough for listing order; the author keeps the exact value). Done as written: truncated to the first of its month for every non-author reader, exact for the author and superusers |
| **Closed 2026-09-23.** `display_name`, `Dataset.name` / `description`, `DatasetFolder.name` and `Tag.name` accepted any text and were shown to grantees, token holders, peers and, for `display_name`, in push-notification bodies; tags were listed to every authenticated user with no access filter | Free text is the classic identifier channel (¶ 23, 50). The platform's whole de-identification effort is bypassed by an uploader who types a name. The collection listing that shows the author their `original_name` where no label is set is correctly gated by `_can_see_original_name` and is not part of this gap; it is named so the next reader does not mistake it for one | Tag listing is scoped to the tags the caller can reach, and creating one is reserved for staff by default; every write of one of these labels returns a heuristic warning when the text looks like a person, an identifier or a date, extended per deployment through `TEXT_HYGIENE_PATTERNS`; push bodies carry the hash-prefix handle only. | **Closed 2026-09-23.** No per-recording record of the de-identification applied: which pass version wrote the stored header, whether annotation text was stripped or preserved (`preserve_annotations` was a transient task argument), which ingest privacy overrides were in force | ¶ 41 requires documentation of the anonymisation process sufficient to demonstrate its effectiveness; `channel_order_version` was the only stamped fact, and a stored file with preserved annotation text was indistinguishable in the database from one without | A `deidentification_version` and a `annotation_text_preserved` flag on `RecordingMeta`, stamped at ingest like `channel_order_version`, and the override settings captured in the READY transition's audit payload alongside the existing `SignalInfo` digest. Done as written, with `deidentification_report` presenting the record; recordings processed before the change carry version `0` and no sealed record, and are reported as such |
| The words "anonymous" and "anonymisation" in two frontend strings, one API error message and the published data-protection page | ¶ 40 directly: these are controller statements to data subjects and recipients | **Closed 2026-09-22.** Reworded throughout; the docs-site page ships with its next release |
| **Closed 2026-09-23.** The annotation export served annotation text verbatim, by role, past the redaction rule, and nothing in the exported document said what it was | Deliberate and documented, but the exported document leaves the platform and nothing marked it as personal data | Done as written: format version 3 carries `data_classification: pseudonymised_personal_data` and the de-identification pass versions of the exported recordings in both formats, documented in [annotations/README.md](../annotations/README.md); `withhold_text=true` applies the redaction rule's own decision to every row the exporter did not write, for exports prepared for a recipient under a de-identifying arrangement. The role gate is unchanged |
| Media and DICOM have no de-identification at all | Personal data by inspection (¶ 50); already recorded in [gdpr-compliance.md](gdpr-compliance.md#known-gaps) and the plugin README | Existing ROADMAP entries. The one addition this assessment makes: neither may be described as covered by "the platform's de-identification" in any notice |
| **Closed 2026-09-22.** `AccessRight.apply_middleware` defaulted to `False` at the model level while every API and command path defaulted it to `True` | A row created through the ORM, by a project plugin, a fixture or a migration, was a raw grant unless the author of that code remembered the flag; the safe default lived one layer up from where the row was made | Done as written: the model default is `True`, the migration rewrites no row, and an explicit `False` is the deliberate act of granting the raw file |
| The serve-time `DeidentifyEDFHeader` and `StripAnnotationTextMiddleware` fail open on a parse error | Documented and deliberate, and safe as long as ingest fails closed on the same input; it means the wire layer is defence in depth rather than an independent control | No change; recorded so the assessment does not credit the wire layer twice |

Two findings sit outside the table because they are positions rather than gaps. The teaching deployment whose stated position is that no patient personal data reaches the platform needs the four ingest privacy overrides on and a written client-side anonymisation step to point at, and even then holds pseudonymised data for any recording that arrived with a signal, since the signal is the identifier. And the outbound JWT `sub` being the local integer primary key lets peers correlate a platform *user* across peers (ROADMAP, federation); it concerns account holders rather than recording subjects and is out of this document's scope.
